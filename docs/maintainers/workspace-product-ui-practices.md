# UI and UX practices worth borrowing from a workspace product

The homepage makes an abstract offer visible. Before visitors have to interpret a feature list, they see a workspace with navigation, a project board or document, and work in progress. The strongest idea to borrow is the sequence. Show what working inside the product feels like, let someone recognize a task of their own, then give them a low-commitment way to try it.

Reviewed November 22, 2026, in Edge at 1,440 × 900 and 390 × 844. I inspected the public homepage, pricing, marketplace, a planner category and template, and a reporting example. I followed the public browsing paths through the template detail page. Two homepage designs appeared across these observations and a November 21 desktop capture. Both the type and button palette changed between versions. The measurements below identify which version they describe.

## Showing the product

- **Make the interface the main visual.** The desktop hero puts a workspace window directly below the invitation. Its sidebar, task columns, and populated records let a visitor recognize what they would actually be working in. [Homepage][home]
- **Use a short headline and give the explanation its own line.** In the blue-button version, one large statement introduces collaborative work. The sentence beneath names three concrete capabilities. The headline stays readable at a glance, and the visitor can decide whether to read on.
- **Show a product change in progress.** In the black-button version, text appears inside a document in the framed preview. That small action explains the product more economically than another paragraph about writing assistance.
- **Give moving demonstrations a visible pause control.** I activated Pause on the homepage and the control changed to Play. A visitor can stop on an interesting frame and inspect it.
- **Use two entry points for different buying situations.** Free access and a demo request appear together, with the first carrying greater visual weight. An individual can explore immediately while a team evaluating a purchase has an equally clear destination.
- **Keep top-level capabilities few enough to remember.** Gathering context, finding answers, and automating tasks organize both the product menu and the page. Visitors encounter the same concepts as they move from navigation into detail.
- **Name use cases by the work someone needs to finish.** Support-ticket resolution, feedback triage, and weekly reporting each lead to a distinct example. A visitor can recognize their own job without learning the product’s terminology.
- **Let visitors inspect the recipe behind a demonstration.** The reporting example includes a sample prompt specifying the source project tracker, what a weekly update contains, and who reviews it. Someone evaluating automation can judge the input, output, and human handoff. [Reporting example][report]
- **Make customer proof inspectable.** Several recognizable customer logos link to individual stories. The customer section adds quotations linked to those stories, giving a skeptical reader somewhere to go beyond recognition. [Homepage][home]
- **Repeat the decision after the evidence.** The closing section brings back free access and the demo request. A visitor who needed to see the examples first can act without returning to the header.

## Typography, color, and composition

- **Give one sans-serif responsibility for most of the hierarchy.** In the blue-button desktop capture, the browser rendered **Inter** for the hero and body. The hero used **96 px / 100 px**, weight **600**, with **−4.6 px** tracking. The supporting sentence used **20 px / 28 px** at **400**. Scale does most of the work. [Homepage][home]
- **Change the voice specifically for a quotation.** In that version, a customer quotation used **Lyon Text Web** at **18 px**, weight **400**. In the earlier alternate design, a closing quotation used **Termoli** at **15 px**, weight **460**. Both reserve a change of voice for someone else’s words.
- **Preserve a display face’s character across sizes.** The black-button version used **Die Grotesk** for its marketing headline. It measured **80 px / 80 px** at **500** in the saved desktop capture and **40 px / 40 px** at **600** in the phone capture. The smaller version gains weight as it gives up size.
- **Use one hue at two strengths to rank actions.** The blue desktop hero put white type on **#0075DE** for its primary action. Its companion used **#005BAB** text on **#E6F3FE**. Both measured **156 × 38 px** with **8 px** corners. Color communicates priority while the common dimensions keep the pair together.
- **Use a neutral action palette when artwork supplies the color.** The other homepage paired **#000000** with **#F6F5F4** for its primary and secondary actions. The phone buttons were **44 px** high and fully rounded. Painted scenery around the demo carried the richer color.
- **Keep illustration outside the working area.** Character drawings peek around the desktop product window, and the alternate version places artwork behind the frame. Navigation, document text, and project records remain easy to inspect.
- **Keep the opening compact enough to reveal the product.** The desktop hero and preview align within a measured **1,252 px** region. On the phone capture the two actions still fit side by side, leaving room for the preview to start in the first screen.
- **Keep the main action outside the mobile menu.** The phone header leaves a compact trial link visible alongside the menu button. A visitor who already knows what they want can start directly.

## Making plans comparable

- **Group tiers by the decision the buyer is making.** The pricing page pairs the two organization-oriented plans and pairs the two aimed at teams needing AI or administrative control. The group headings help a visitor decide which pair deserves a closer read. [Pricing][pricing]
- **Give each plan the same reading order.** Name, unit price, intended user, action, and included features occupy predictable positions. The enterprise tier identifies custom pricing and offers a sales contact in the corresponding slots.
- **Update figures where the visitor is comparing them.** I switched from yearly to monthly billing in USD. The two paid self-service cards changed from **$10 and $20** to **$12 and $24** per member per month. Switching back restored both figures. The billing choice and price unit were visible together.
- **Make a recommendation legible without relying on color.** A text badge identifies the recommended tier. Its stronger action and lightly tinted group reinforce that recommendation while the other prices stay visible.
- **Describe what an upgrade adds.** Higher plans introduce their lists by identifying the preceding plan they include. Buyers can scan for the capability that justifies the extra spend.
- **Offer a deeper comparison after the short one.** The overview supports a quick choice. The feature matrix below handles questions such as page history and administrative controls, so the first row of cards can stay concise.
- **Explain the billing edge case beside the buying decision.** Expanding the question about changing team size revealed how additions are prorated and what happens to a removed member’s seat. A growing team gets a more practical answer than a billing-period label alone.

## Helping visitors find a starting point

- **Support both search and discovery.** The marketplace places a named template-search control alongside curated collections and categories. Visitors who can name their task can search, while others can browse examples of what is possible. [Marketplace][market]
- **Show how much lies behind a category.** In this visit the weekly-planner category showed **858 templates** before I opened it. The count helps a visitor distinguish a broad collection from a single example.
- **Put filters where choices multiply.** Inside a category, controls expose creator source, free or paid availability, and sort order. Choosing Free opened a dedicated URL for free weekly planners, so a visitor can share that narrower collection. [Planner category][category]
- **Give reusable work a visual preview and a price label.** Template tiles pair an image of the artifact with a title and price or free label. A visitor can evaluate both fit and cost while scanning.
- **Separate inspection from acquisition.** The planner I opened had distinct controls for viewing the template and getting it. A guest can investigate the artifact before deciding whether it belongs in their workspace. [Template detail][detail]
- **Give the artifact provenance.** The detail page links the creator, shows when the item was last updated, and describes what the recipient gets. Those facts help answer whether the template is maintained and usable.
- **Put the sample size beside social proof.** That planner showed **21 ratings**, with a distribution by star count. The reader can judge how much evidence sits behind the displayed score.
- **Offer relevant alternatives at the decision point.** The template page ends with other planners and related guidance. Someone who likes the task but wants a different layout can keep exploring without reconstructing the search.
